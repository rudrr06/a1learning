Switching & the MAC Address Table

A switch is a smart post office for one network. Press Next step and watch it learn.

What just happened?

The step you are on lights up here.

  1. 0. Empty table – A new switch knows nothing. The MAC table maps each MAC address to a port.
  2. 1. A sends a frame – Frames carry a source and destination MAC address (Layer 2 addresses).
  3. 2. Learn the source – The switch always reads the source MAC of incoming frames and records the port it came from.
  4. 3. Unknown → flood – If the destination is not in the table, the switch copies the frame to every other port. Only the real owner accepts it.
  5. 4. Learn from the reply – B's reply teaches the switch where BB lives. After a short chat, the table is complete.
  6. 5. Forward to one port – Known destination: the frame goes only to that port. This is what makes switches better than hubs.
  7. 6. MAC flooding – The table has limited memory. An attacker overflows it with fake source addresses.
  8. 7. Fail-open – With a full table the switch floods everything, so an attacker can sniff other people's traffic.
  9. 8. Port security – Limit the number of MACs per port and shut down or alert on violations.

Why it matters

Switching is how every office, home and data-centre LAN works. Understanding the MAC table explains why LAN traffic is normally private and fast.

Security: MAC flooding, ARP spoofing and sniffing all attack the trust a switch places in source addresses. Port security, 802.1X and network monitoring are the standard defences.
