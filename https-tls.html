HTTPS & the TLS Handshake

The padlock in your browser is the result of this handshake. (Simplified, based on TLS 1.3.)

What just happened?

The step you are on lights up here.

  1. 1. ClientHello – The browser offers its capabilities.
  2. 2. ServerHello + certificate – The server chooses settings and proves its identity.
  3. 3. Verify – The browser validates the certificate chain.
  4. 4. Shared key – Both sides compute the same secret session key.
  5. 5. Encrypted data – HTTP is now protected from eavesdropping and tampering.

Why it matters

What TLS gives you: confidentiality (encryption), integrity (data cannot be changed unnoticed) and authentication (you are talking to the real domain). Asymmetric cryptography and certificates establish trust and the key, then fast symmetric encryption protects the data.

Forward secrecy: modern TLS uses temporary keys for each session, so recording traffic today and stealing the server's private key later does not reveal old conversations.

Security link: the padlock means the connection is encrypted to that domain, not that the site is trustworthy: phishing sites have padlocks too. A man-in-the-middle cannot silently read HTTPS because they cannot present a valid certificate for the domain, which is why certificate warnings must never be ignored. Old protocols (SSL, TLS 1.0 and 1.1) are broken and should be disabled.
